What this is

The mushroom body is a sparse expansion in the fly brain: a handful of projection neurons fan out onto thousands of Kenyon cells, which then vote in four compartments. We never told this network what a pair is. Hand strength is supposed to emerge from random sparse conjunctions of raw cards, chip counts, and the last few actions.

Phase A (offline) copies a teacher that is allowed to use equity. The fly only sees cards and bets. When it is wrong, dopamine depresses synapses for the correct action and potentiates the action it chose (Hige et al. 2015; Handler et al. 2019). Phase B is what you are doing now: after the hand, a delayed reward lands on an eligibility trace — synapses that were active when it acted, decaying through the hand. That is how a showdown can train decisions made streets earlier. The online rate is far below the offline rate, and the bootstrap weights stay as an immutable base. Use the reset control if you want the stock fly back.

Limits, plainly: it has no search, no working memory across hands beyond the learned weights, and no theory of mind. It does not bluff strategically — anything that looks like a bluff is a learned association, not a plan. It plays a reasonable tight game and will adapt to obvious habits; a strong player will beat it. Only the mushroom body is simulated. The dopamine-gated plasticity is our addition, not part of the published model.

This is play money, for fun. A game is 500 chips each until one side is broke. There is nothing to buy and no leaderboard.
